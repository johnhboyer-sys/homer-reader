  43. Batieia, the barrow of Myrine

    Βατίεια speculative

    'There is before the city a steep mound out in the plain, standing clear with a way round it on either side, which men call Batieia but the immortals the barrow of much-leaping Myrine' (2.811-814) — the double naming, men's word against the gods', marks old material in the poem. It is where the Trojan and allied contingents muster before the battle begins. Hainsworth holds that the Catalogue of Ships works from a different topography of the plain from the battle books, referring for it to the notes on 2.793 and 2.811-815 (Commentary III, 243) - and 2.811-815 is this record's only Homeric position, as 2.793 is the tomb of Aesyetes'. The schematic anchor stands where the poem's own words put it, in the plain in front of the city and clear of everything round it, with the warning attached rather than the anchor moved.

    Tradition: A tumulus on the Trojan plain, discussed among the tombs of the plain since Strabo (13.1.36-37); no ancient or modern survey, including Leaf (1912), matches a surviving mound to it

    Il. 2.811–815

  44. Beşik Bay

    certain

    A bay on the Aegean coast southwest of Troy, facing Tenedos. Kraft, Rapp, Kayan and Luce 2003 read it as a harbor: the Beşik embayment 'always provided a place of shelter for ships' against the Dardanelles current, an important roadstead even after the Troian bay had silted. Korfmann's 1982-87 excavation found a cemetery of roughly 100 graves here in which Mycenaean and Mycenaeanising LH IIIB fine ware runs to about a third of the wares — against under 1% at Troia VI/VII itself — making it the strongest archaeological candidate for a working Late Bronze Age port. No survey coordinate for the bay as a whole; the sheet letters it as a water name over the modern sea, anchored from Beşik Tepe at the bay's north end, 39.9171 / 26.1591.

  45. Beşik-Sivritepe, claimed tomb of Achilles

    traditional

    A conical tumulus at the north end of Beşik Bay, about 2 km SSW of Yeniköy; its present cone is Hellenistic (Rose and Körpe 2016). The town of Achilleion was the Mytileneans' base in their war with Athens over Sigeion (Herodotus 5.94) and, by Pliny's account, their foundation (Natural History 5.125). The rival localization is at Cape Sigeion: Strabo puts a temple and monument of Achilles near Sigeion (13.1.32), and from Lechevalier's map of 1791 the travelers marked the tomb on a tumulus there (Burgess), which this sheet does not draw. Arrian has Alexander garland the tomb of Achilles (Anabasis 1.12.1) but does not say which mound.

    Tradition: One of the rival tombs of Achilles (Burgess, "Tumuli of Achilles"): the tumulus beside Achilleion, the town Pliny puts next to the tomb (Natural History 5.125), with Achilleion placed on the neighboring cape of Beşika Burnu and its mound, Beşik-Yassıtepe (Pleiades 550401)

  53. Callicolone, 'Fair Hill'

    Καλλικολώνη traditional

    A rise beside the Simoeis where Ares rallies the Trojans (20.53) and the pro-Trojan gods take their seats to watch the battle of the gods (20.151), the Trojan counterpart to the Achaean wall where the pro-Greek gods sit (20.144-148). Pinned at Kara Tepe (editorial ruling, 2026-07-30): the surveyed peak 8.5 km east of Troy, elevation 207 m — Spratt's own 680 ft, per Cook 110 — carrying the Spratt/Forchhammer identification Cook defends and Luce adopts; the support is judged overwhelming and the tier raised accordingly. Leaf instead proposed Ophrynion, on the plain's opposite side, and stays in the record as the named dissent; Strabo's forty stades sit between the two positions.

    Tradition: Kara Tepe, roughly 8.5 km east of Troy near the Simoeis: identified by Spratt and Forchhammer (19th c.), defended by J. M. Cook (1973, 110-13) working from Strabo's stade-figures (13.1.35: 40 stadia from Ilium, 5 from the Simoeis), and adopted by Luce (1984) — the consensus tradition; Leaf (1912, 44) instead proposed Ophrynion, on the plain's opposite side

  68. Chryse

    Χρύση speculative

    The town of Chryses, priest of Apollo, whose daughter Agamemnon seizes and whose anger opens the poem. Already lost by Strabo's day: he places Homer's Chryse at the ancient, deserted town near Thebe, and treats the Chrysa near Hamaxitos — where the cult of Apollo Smintheus was later re-established — as the view of "those less acquainted with ancient history". The Barrington Atlas carries both candidates, the Chrysa on the Gulf of Adramyttium near Thebe and the Smintheion site, plus a third entry that is simply unlocated.

    Tradition: Strabo 13.1.63 locates Homer's Chryse at the ancient, by-then-deserted town near Thebe, not at the later Chrysa near Hamaxitos to which the temple of Apollo Smintheus was transferred after the Cilicians were driven out; Strabo names the Hamaxitos identification the view of "those less acquainted with ancient history"

  291. The Bay of Troy (the silted embayment)

    certain

    Not named by Homer, but the single most important thing to get right on the map. A marine embayment once reached roughly 10 km south of Hisarlık at maximum Holocene transgression (Kraft, Kayan, and Erol 1980); the ria intruded some 17 km up the lower Karamenderes valley by 7000–6000 BP (Kayan); progradation had carried the coast west of Troia by c. 4000 BP; and a 2–2.5 m Late Bronze Age sea-level fall left a shallow lagoon behind a wide sandy barrier, with swamp over much of the delta.   305. The Kesik basin / Kesik cut

    certain

    A trough through the coastal ridge west of the plain, at the ridge's lowest point about 500 m south of Kesik Tepe; Cook puts its floor perhaps 12–15 m above sea level (Cook 1973, 166), and Kayan's survey puts the bedrock threshold at 13 m (Kayan 2009). East of the ridge lies the Kesik plain, the 'Lisgar' marsh of Spratt's map, which Cook found drained and cultivated (Cook 1973, 166). Drilling shows a small marine inlet there that had silted to swamp by about 4000–3500 BP, too early for a harbor in the time of Troia VI (Kayan 2009). Cook took the trough for an artificial drainage cut, offered the foundation of Constantine's new city as the occasion, and judged that 'the work was never completed' (Cook 1973, 167). Kayan's three studies agree that the cut was never a waterway. In 1995 Kayan judged that it probably began on a fault line and was then perhaps dug out by degrees by people who used it as a passage (Kayan 1995, 226–27). Kayan and his colleagues in 2003 took its shape to imply digging, called its purpose and date unknown, and offered either gradual opening by the people of Sigeion and later Yenihisar or an unfinished canal (Kayan et al. 2003, 399). In 2009 Kayan grants that its straight line looks artificial but finds no sign that it was dug and no spoil, and judges its most probable origin a fault line formed before the Holocene (Kayan 2009, 108–9, 123–25). Luce read the cut as the Achaean wall and ditch (Luce 1998); Kraft, Rapp, Kayan and Luce 2003 print that reading with a citation to Kayan 1995, which does not contain it. Zangger argues for artificial harbor works at Troy (Zangger, "Searching for the Ports of Troy"); with Mutlu he proposes, as a working hypothesis, a port basin linked to the Aegean by a slipway (Zangger and Mutlu 2015). Kraft, Kayan, Brückner and Rapp 2003 grant only that the embayments below the Sigeion ridge "had excellent harbor potentials." The schematic sheet does not draw or letter the feature.

  310. The pyre of Patroclus

    πυρὴ Πατρόκλου speculative

  325. The two fair-flowing springs of the Scamander

    κρουνὼ καλλιρρόω speculative
