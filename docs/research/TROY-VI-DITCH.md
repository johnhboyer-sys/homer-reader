## 1. The survey grids, and how to read a square as meters

Two systems are used interchangeably for the same ground (p. 680): the **Dörpfeld
system**, which divides the area into 20 m squares with letters for columns and
numbers for rows, and the **running coordinates of the modern survey**, introduced
by Eberhard Messmer, printed as `N ...` for northings and `E ...` for eastings.

**Rotation.** Footnote 23, p. 680: "Messmer 1992, 71. Hier muß hervorgehoben
werden, daß sowohl das Dörpfeld-Raster als auch das dazu angepaßte aktuelle
Koordinatensystem um 5,509 Grad gegen geographisch Nord (wGS84) verdreht ist." So
both grids are rotated **5.509° from geographic north (WGS84)**, after **Messmer,
Eberhard. "Grundlagenvermessung als Basis für ein archäologisches
Informationssystem in Troia." *Studia Troica* 2 (1992): 71–82.**

### 1.1 Rows — confirmed, and stated more exactly than before

Read off the margin of Abb. 13 at 1200 dpi: the horizontal 100 m line labelled
`x=10800` runs along the **bottom edge** of the box for row 60, and `x=10700`
along the bottom edge of row 5. Therefore

> **row *n* (n = 1…47) spans northing 10820 − 20*n* down to 10800 − 20*n*;
> its center is N = 10810 − 20*n*.**
> Rows 55–60 continue north of row 1: south edge = 12000 − 20*n*
> (row 60 = 10820–10800, row 55 = 10920–10900).

This **corrects** `RESEARCH-CITADEL.md` §2 Claim 4, which recorded the formula as
"row *n* sits at x = 10800 − 20·n". That value is the row's **southern edge**, not
the row. The difference is 10–20 m and it decides square membership. Two
independent checks in the chapter's own prose settle it:

- p. 687: the first stretch of the ditch found is "im Bereich N 10230, E 9170 bis
  E 9270", and the sondage that tested it was "in den Quadraten **z29 und A29**".
  Row 29 = N 10240–10220 under the rule above, so N 10230 is the middle of row 29.
  Under the old reading row 29 would be N 10220–10200 and the trench would have
  missed the anomaly by 10–30 m.
- p. 684: a building "bei **N 10340**, E 9150", tested by "eine Sondage im
  Quadranten **x24**". Row 24 = N 10340–10320: N 10340 is its northern edge.

### 1.2 Columns — the open item, now closed

`RESEARCH-CITADEL.md` §7 lists "the column half of the grid mapping" as **not
resolved**, and §6 item 1 asks for Messmer 2014 Abb. 2 at print resolution to
settle it. It is settled here from Abb. 13's own bottom margin, read at 1200 dpi:
the vertical 100 m line `y=8700` falls exactly on the **zz | a** column boundary,
`y=8800` on **e | f**, `y=8900` on **k | l**, `y=9000` on **p | q**.

> **Column at sequence index *i* spans easting 8700 + 20*i* to 8720 + 20*i*;
> its center is E = 8710 + 20*i*.**
> The sequence is a b c d e f g h i k l m n o p q r s t u v w x y z (i = 0…24,
> **no j**), then A…Z with no J (25…49), then AA…ZZ with no JJ (50…74), then
> AAA BBB CCC. Going west: zz = −1 (8680–8700), yy = −2.

Checks in the chapter's prose:

- p. 684: the building "bei N 10340, **E 9150**" sits in square **x24**. x is
  index 22, spanning E 9140–9160, so E 9150 is the middle of column x.
- p. 685: a wall widening "bei **mn15** (N 10500, **E 8950**)". m = 8920–8940,
  n = 8940–8960: E 8950 falls in n, on the m/n pair the square name gives.
- p. 687: the anomaly "E 9170 bis E 9270" was tested in **z29 and A29**;
  z = 9180–9200, A = 9200–9220, both inside that span.

## 2. What the chapter says about the Late Bronze Age ditches

Section "Spätbronzezeitliche Schichten (Troia VI/VII) — die Felsgräben", pp. 686–88,
plus the summary on 666–67 and the follow-up methods on 689–90. Page numbers on
every line.

### 2.1 The cutting itself

- **p. 666–67 (Zusammenfassung / Abstract).** Over about **700 m** the course of a
  ditch cut into the rock was located, **up to 3 m wide and about 1.5 m deep**, of
  the Late Bronze Age (**Troia VI/VII**), interpreted as an approach obstacle
  protecting the lower settlement. The findings were verified by targeted
  excavation sondages. The prospected area is about **0.75 km²** south and east of
  the citadel mound; measured 1989–2007 at a mean of eight readings per m² by
  teams from four institutions. (The 0.75 km² is the *survey* area, not the area
  the ditch encloses; the chapter gives no figure for the latter.)
- **p. 687.** First seen in the area **N 10230, E 9170 to E 9270** — a length of
  100 m — as an irregular, somewhat diffuse positive anomaly; first modelled as a
  mudbrick wall on the rock (Becker et al. 1993, 122, Abb. 3, 5–6). The following
  sondage in squares **z29 and A29** proved that wrong: the rock stood at 0.5–1 m
  depth, and the anomaly came not from masonry but from **a ditch cut into the
  calcareous sandstone, about 3 m wide and up to 2 m deep**, later filled with Late
  Bronze Age topsoil of high magnetic susceptibility washed in by erosion — which is
  what produces the magnetic contrast. **The fill was identified unambiguously as
  Troy VI in date** (Jablonka, Koenig and Riehl, *Studia Troica* 4, 1994, 51–73,
  cited as Jablonka et al. 1994, 53–66, Abb. 1–2).
- **p. 687.** The rock ditch was then prospected over a length of **340 m in a
  west–east direction** (Becker – Jansen 1994, 106–107, Abb. 2–3).
- **p. 687.** Two rectangular raster structures once read as the foundations of a
  stone-box wall following the ditch **could not be verified**; they proved to be
  **Byzantine workings in the rock** (Jablonka 1995, 41).
- **p. 687.** The chapter explicitly **declines to enter** the argument over the
  archaeological interpretation — "die nicht einhellig akzeptierte archäologische
  Interpretation als Verteidigungsgraben oder Annäherungshindernis für
  spätbronzezeitliche Streitwagen" — and leaves open whether remains of a rampart
  or wall stood on the citadel side of the ditch. **The function is contested; the
  cutting, its date and its course are what the survey establishes.**

### 2.2 The gate passage

- **p. 687.** The anomaly is **interrupted at two places**, each over a length of
  some meters; footnote 83 (Becker – Jansen 1994, Abb. 4) puts the interruptions in
  squares **y29 and n28**. Excavation at one of them, square **y 28/29**, proved
  the interruption of the ditch, and **behind it the postholes of palisade
  structures**. The finding is interpreted as a **Tordurchlaß — a gate passage**
  (Jablonka 1996, 68, Abb. 2).

### 2.3 The west and north

- **p. 687.** In the west the ditch **bends to the north-west in square n28**.
  After about **120 m**, in square **i25**, a change of direction **to the
  north-east** is indistinctly visible, pointing toward the citadel hill (state
  after the third campaign, 1994; Korfmann 1995, 5–7 with H. Becker's report).
- **p. 687.** After prospection resumed with a cesium magnetometer in 1998, the
  aim was the ditch's continuation on the western side. Next to the strong, long
  negative trace of the Hellenistic city wall **no clear positive lineament of the
  ditch running north can be found at first**. Only from **(N 10510, E 8980)** does
  a very weak positive linear anomaly run **in a shallow arc to the north**.
- **p. 688.** In square **p12** it cuts the negative anomaly of the Hellenistic
  city wall. A sondage was laid there, and **the finding of a Late Bronze Age
  (Troy VI) filled rock ditch was confirmed here too** (Korfmann 2001, 27, Abb. 24).
- **p. 688.** **"Im Bereich dazwischen ist der genaue Verlauf dieses Grabens nicht
  einwandfrei belegt, da mehrere positive Lineamente nebeneinander auftauchen."**
  In the ground between, the exact course is not established, because several
  positive lineaments appear side by side. Nevertheless the assumption is plausible
  that **in late Troy VI the ditch marked the outer boundary of the Bronze Age
  settlement area**.
- **p. 688.** A junction of the inner Bronze Age ditch with the younger outer
  system **in the area N 10320, E 8900** was assumed at first and presented at the
  Heidelberg symposium of 2001, and was used argumentatively afterward. A later
  detailed analysis of the anomalies led to a **re-evaluation of the inner ditch's
  course, with a bend to the north**, which the Troia project has used in its
  publications since. **The old junction is superseded and must not be drawn.**

### 2.4 The east and north-east

- **p. 688.** In the east, magnetic prospection **hit an insurmountable limit**
  trying to determine the further course east or north beyond the still
  unambiguous finding in square **F28**: the magnetic disturbances of the
  shallower Hellenistic-Roman foundation remains completely cover the weak Bronze
  Age ones. Geoelectrics, electromagnetic measurement and ground radar all failed
  as well, on the properties of the strongly water-bearing, horizontally bedded
  limestone.
- **p. 690.** Georadar could not find the Bronze Age ditch at all: **the fill
  absorbs the signal completely**, so nothing reflects (tried 1997 and 2004–06).
  Eleven geoelectric soundings north of **G27** in 2008 could not fix the
  continuation either.
- **p. 690.** In 2000 the EM-31 was carried into part of square **G27** looking for
  the Troy VI ditch and gave a promising conductivity anomaly. **The excavation of
  2006, just north of that anomaly, proved the ditch was there** (Jablonka –
  Pernicka 2007, 10–16).
- **p. 689.** The attempts to locate the ditch north-eastward — "der für das
  Verständnis der spätbronzezeitlichen Untersiedlung von großer Bedeutung ist" —
  are therefore left to conventional methods: a close net of borings for the rock
  profile paired with clarifying sondages.

### 2.5 The outer ditch

- **p. 688.** In **1994**, south-west outside the inner area, at square **g28**, a
  further ditch anomaly was prospected (Korfmann 1995, 7). The sondage that
  followed found **another U-shaped structure cut into the rock, identified by its
  fill as Troia VII in date, thus younger than the known "inner" ditch**. This was
  then **overlaid in Roman times by a V-shaped ditch**. The double finding makes it
  hard to assign the several positive anomalies to one ditch or the other. It does
  not seem far-fetched to assume here **an extension of the ditch structure as the
  settled area grew in Troia VII**.
- **p. 686.** The same square again: into the fill of the second Late Bronze Age
  ditch, "so der Befund in der Sondage g28", a probably water-carrying V-shaped
  ditch was cut in Roman times.
- **Abb. 13** labels it **"Äußerer Graben (Troia VIIa?)"** and draws it green,
  distinct from the red "Innerer Graben (Troia VI)". The question mark is the
  chapter's.

### 2.6 What else the survey found, and did not

- **p. 689.** Apart from the ditch structures, the magnetogram gave **no hint of
  Bronze Age building structures anywhere** — probably because the Bronze Age
  buildings, apart from the stone houses near the citadel, were of perishable
  material. On the much-argued question of where the cemeteries lie, which one
  would expect outside the ditch, magnetic prospection gives **no reliable
  answer**; only south of the Late Bronze Age cemetery Blegen investigated do
  cloudy anomalies in otherwise sterile sediment, in the area **N 10060–10100,
  E 9150–9200**, hint at possible grave structures, still untested.

## 4. Georeference: survey → WGS84

### 4.1 The tie is published, and it is used unmodified

`RESEARCH-CITADEL.md` §2 already carries an absolute tie: a four-parameter
Helmert fit of 36 coordinate pairs printed in Messmer's Tab. 2 ("Koordinatenliste
Troia 2000"), taking the Schliemann-Dörpfeld-Koordinatensystem 1987 (SDK87) to
Gauß-Krüger on the WGS84 ellipsoid, central meridian 27°:

```
scale     1.0000602
rotation  5.01987°
tx        426 605.463        ty  4 413 614.892      (GK metres, zone prefix removed)
residuals mean 3 mm, max 12 mm, n = 36
```

Applied as

```
E_gk = tx + s·( y·cos θ − x·sin θ )
N_gk = ty + s·( x·cos θ + y·sin θ )          x = SDK87 northing, y = SDK87 easting
```

it reproduces the one raw pair §2 prints — point 100014, SDK87 (x 10599.48,
y 9639.234) ↔ GK (435 280.779, 4 425 017.849) — to **1 mm** in both axes, which
also fixes the **sign**: SDK87 grid north lies **west** of Gauß-Krüger grid north.
An inverse transverse Mercator (WGS84, CM 27°, k₀ = 1, FE 500 000) then gives
lat/lon. That is the whole chain the plate uses. No hand-fitted shift is applied.

### 4.2 The two rotation figures are the same rotation

The brief for this lane assumed the chapter's **5.509°** superseded §2's
**5.01987°** as an unverified number. It does not. They measure the rotation
against **different norths** and differ by the meridian convergence at Troy:

```
γ = (λ − λ₀)·sin φ = (26.2390 − 27)·sin 39.9570° = −0.4887°
5.01987° + 0.4887° = 5.5086°   ≈   5.509°   (chapter, p. 680 n. 23)
```

So: SDK87 grid north is **5.509° west of true north** and **5.020° west of
Gauß-Krüger grid north**, and the two published figures corroborate each other to
within 0.0004°. §2's fit is not superseded; it is confirmed, and the sign that was
never stated is now fixed. Recorded in `RESEARCH-CITADEL.md` §2.

### 4.3 The residual against the plate's drawn citadel

`apparatus/plates/troy-citadel.json`'s Dörpfeld circuit was placed on the
schematic sheet's Ilios center, **(39.95700, 26.23900)**, without rotation. Run
backward through §4.1 that point is

> **SDK87 x (northing) 10640.3, y (easting) 9335.1.**

Compare §3: it falls **inside** the Troy VI circuit as Abb. 13 draws it (E 9190–9405,
N 10635–10755), **66 m** from that circuit's bounding-box center at (N 10695,
E 9298) — 55 m south and 37 m east of it.

**That 66 m is the residual, and it is left in.** Sliding the whole ditch by 66 m
to make a hand-placed sheet center agree with a ±15 m reading of a printed figure
would trade a published coordinate for a fitted one; the reading tolerance and the
softness of "center" for a circuit whose north side is destroyed together cover
most of it. If a later lane wants the ditch aligned to the drawn citadel instead
of to the ground, the correction is one constant: add **N +55 m, E −37 m** to
every survey coordinate in §5 before transforming.

At the "Before the walls" panel's scale (about 0.56 px per meter) 66 m is ~37 px;
on the map face it is under 2 px.

## 6. Why this is fact-extraction and not a trace

`RESEARCH-CITADEL.md` §3.2 records the posture: the chapter is in copyright,
"extracting the ditch's *course* as coordinates and redrawing it is arguably
fact-extraction, not copying; tracing the *line* produces a derivative of their
cartography." The queue entry (item 15) says the same: "read, cite facts, do not
trace."

What was done, concretely:

- **28 vertices in all**, for a course the published line renders in hundreds. Nine
  of them are coordinates or squares the chapter states in prose; the rest are
  positions read against the printed grid at a stated ±10 m.
- No pixel of the published figure was sampled, vectorized or offset. The figure
  was rendered and *read*, the way one reads a value off a printed graph.
- The chapter's own distinction between an established course and an interpolated
  one is preserved (solid vs. dotted), because that distinction is a claim, not a
  drawing style.
- The one stretch that would have needed the most figure-reading and has the least
  textual support — the north-east continuation, §5.7 — is recorded here and left
  off the plate.

The chapter itself warns (p. 679) that the printed magnetogram is heavily
compressed and "nicht gut geeignet" as a basis for planning, and directs anyone
needing precision to the project GIS. That is a further argument for taking
recorded positions at a declared tolerance rather than pretending to the
figure's own precision.

the lower city, Troy VI"**, which puts its numeral inside the panel and keeps a
name off the map face, per rulings 6 and 10.
