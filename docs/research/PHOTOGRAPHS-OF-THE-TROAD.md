# PHOTOGRAPHS-OF-THE-TROAD.md — what the ground actually looks like

**Date:** 2026-08-14. **Commissioned by:** John's question after seeing the raised-oblique
"Ships, the Bay, and Ilios" plate — "aren't there photographs from a similar vantage?"
**Purpose:** find real photographs of the Troad and extract FACTS ABOUT APPEARANCE
(colour, texture, haze, how the citadel reads at distance) to check the plate against.
**Not done:** no image was downloaded, embedded, traced or reproduced. Every claim below
is a description, checked against the cited page; the owner should open the URLs himself.

**Our camera, for reference:** 39.9755 N, 26.1785 E (Sigeion ridge), heading 104°, 72°
horizontal FOV, eye 800 m up, looking toward Hisarlık ~7 km out and Mount Ida ~66 km out.

## 2. Findings on appearance, by question

### 2.1 Ground colour, and how it changes with season
The plain is **intensively cultivated today** — cotton, wheat and tomatoes, per a Turkish
soil-fertility survey of the "Çanakkale–Kumkale Plain" (the Troy-area floodplain) that
sampled 51 points across it. "Modern Troy.jpg" (Brian Harrington Spier, 2007, CC-BY-SA)
is captioned in the photographer's own words: "a view of modern Troy and its **citrus
groves** from the ruins of the ancient city." That is green, not sand-and-sage, and it is
seasonal: cotton and tomatoes are summer-green and go to pale gold/brown at autumn
harvest; wheat is spring-green and straw-gold by early summer; citrus stays dark green
year-round. **None of this is in our plate**, which uses one warm monochrome 12-step tan
ramp (`#EDEEDF`→`#AF9164`, `scripts/panorama-render-stage1d.py:146-149`) with no green
step at any point and no seasonal variable at all.
Before 20th-century irrigation the ground would have read differently but still not as a
uniform sheet: `docs/research/RESEARCH-PALEOGEOGRAPHY.md` §1.1 records swamp mapped along
the Kesik/Yeniköy plain and the Scamander's western and south-western flanks as late as
1980's Fig. 2 — a wetter, darker patch against drier steppe, not one tone.

### 2.2 How flat does the plain read, and what gives it interest
Every photograph we found gets its visual interest from **linework the terrain itself
doesn't have**: field boundaries, canal and river threads, rows of trees along irrigation
channels, and tone breaks between neighbouring crops. The 2022 drone-survey blog is
explicit about this even while arguing the opposite point about elevation: it describes
"a row of small fields on the east side of the canal" and treats the canal itself as a
navigation landmark across an otherwise textureless walk. "Troy view of surroundings
2736.jpg" (Dosseman, 2006, CC-BY-SA 4.0) reads as scattered vegetation of "varying
density" breaking up the ground, not bare dirt. **Our plate has no field, canal, road, or
vegetation-texture layer at all** — `panorama-render-stage1d.py`'s fill pass only ever
paints one of: sea, lagoon, an elevation-banded relief colour, or a contour hairline. The
"featureless dome" John flagged is not an illusion of the render; the pipeline genuinely
draws nothing else on the plain's surface.

### 2.3 Vegetation — scrub, bare ground, cultivation, trees
Cultivated bottomland (cotton/wheat/tomatoes, citrus near the site) dominates the valley
floor; Paolo Monti's 1962 photograph of the "Tumulo di Aiace" (Tomb of Ajax) shows "sparse
vegetation" on the drier ground around the tumulus itself, away from the irrigated
lowland — i.e., a real distinction between scrubby high ground and green bottomland that
our uniform ramp erases. Trees cluster along canals, roads, and the modern village of
Tevfikiye near the site, not scattered evenly across the plain.

### 2.4 How Hisarlık reads at 7 km
This is the one question the sources answer only partially. "Troy view of mound
(1991).jpg" and the drone blog both describe the mound as a **real, if modest, positive
feature** — "clearly visible," "dominating the landscape" in the blog's words — but
neither source states the exact viewing distance, and none of our references is shot from
7 km on the Sigeion ridge specifically. Hisarlık's citadel stands roughly 30 m above a
plain that sits at 1–5 m elevation near the site (`RESEARCH-PALEOGEOGRAPHY.md` §1.1, Fig.
2 spot heights). At 7 km with an 800 m eye, basic geometry says that relief should still
clear the horizon and read as a distinct hummock rather than vanish — but this is
inference from elevation figures, not something I have seen confirmed in a photograph
taken from the matching distance and angle. **Flag as an open question**, not a settled
finding: if this matters for the plate's credibility, the next research pass should try
to find or commission a photograph from closer to 7 km on the Sigeion side specifically.

### 2.5 Haze — how much, and what colour
Every landscape photo we examined shows real atmospheric haze softening the horizon: "a
noticeable atmospheric haze is present, softening distant features" ("Troy view of
surroundings 2736.jpg," Dec. 2006); "distant mountains visible… under what appears to be
atmospheric haze" ("Plain of Troas (Troy) Turkey.jpg," Oct. 2007). In every case the haze
reads as a pale, warm-to-neutral wash, not a saturated blue veil. Schliemann's Plate V
(1874) is captioned with a correction that the original German text called the snow on
Mount Ida "perpetual" and the author struck that word — meaning even at the much shorter
Hisarlık-to-Ida distance, Ida read as intermittently, not permanently, snow-capped, which
tells you the mountain was visible enough to argue about but far enough to be a background
shape, not a crisp foreground presence.

### 2.6 Mount Ida at 66 km
No source we found is shot from 66 km specifically. Schliemann's Plate V (from Hisarlık,
much closer than our Sigeion camera) shows Ida as a background chain, real but secondary,
per its own caption and the "perpetual snow" correction noted above. At nearly double that
distance from Sigeion, Ida should be fainter still — plausibly at or near the edge of
naked-eye visibility except on very clear days. This is an extrapolation from the nearer
view, not a directly observed fact at 66 km, and should be reported to John as such.

## 3. What our plate gets wrong

1. **Ground colour has no green, ever.** The light-theme relief ramp
   (`panorama-render-stage1d.py:146-149`) runs `#EDEEDF → #AF9164`, a warm off-white to tan
   gradient with **zero** green or blue-green step. Every modern photograph we found shows
   cultivated green (cotton, wheat, citrus) covering most of the valley floor most of the
   year. Sand-and-sage may be defensible for a hypothetical pre-agricultural surface but
   is not what a camera would see today, and the plate does not claim to be reconstructing
   an ancient ground cover — it is rendering the modern terrain surface with modern shading.
2. **No texture on the flat ground at all.** No field boundaries, no canal or minor
   watercourse lines, no tree rows, no road. The pipeline's fill pass paints only sea,
   lagoon, elevation-banded relief colour, and contour hairlines
   (`panorama-render-stage1d.py:540-562`) — there is no vegetation or land-use layer to
   draw from, so the "featureless dome" is not a rendering artifact, it is the literal
   absence of a data layer. Every photograph's visual interest on flat ground comes from
   exactly this kind of linework.
3. **Haze doesn't reach far enough, and its colour is unverified.** `haze_cuts` in
   `scripts/panorama-stage2-fragment.py:612` only covers 30000 / 12000 / 5000 m; Mount Ida
   at 66 km lies entirely outside all three strata, so nothing beyond 30 km gets any
   atmospheric attenuation at all, while every photograph we found shows continuous
   softening all the way to the visible horizon. The haze colour is the page background
   (`var(--page-bg)`, a neutral grey in light theme, near-black in dark theme) laid on at
   increasing opacity — directionally the right idea (soften and desaturate with distance)
   but not verified against any photograph's actual haze hue, which read pale and
   warm-to-neutral rather than a flat grey wash.
4. **Hisarlık's read at distance is unresolved, not wrong** — see §2.4. Don't over-correct
   this one without better evidence; the geometry suggests the mound should clear the
   horizon at 7 km, which argues against making it fainter, not for it.
