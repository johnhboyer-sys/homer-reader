## 1. What the *Iliad* itself says Troy looked like

### 1.1 Method

This section combes the poem for **architectural and visual** detail of the city of Ilios /
Troy — buildings, walls, towers, gates, streets, materials, epithets that constrain form.
Topographic relations outside the wall (oak, fig tree, springs, ford, camp) belong to
`RESEARCH-POEM-TOPOGRAPHY.md` and are cited here only when they fix something about the
city's silhouette or immediate approach.

Format for each item:

> **Greek** | **book.line** | **what it licenses an artist to draw**

### 1.2 Priam's house (the fullest architectural description in the poem)

> Ἀλλ' ὅτε δὴ Πριάμοιο δόμον περικαλλέ' ἵκανε / ξεστῇς αἰθούσῃσι τετυγμένον· αὐτὰρ ἐν αὐτῷ / πεντήκοντ' ἔνεσαν θάλαμοι ξεστοῖο λίθοιο / πλησίον ἀλλήλων δεδμημένοι, ἔνθα δὲ παῖδες / κοιμῶντο Πριάμοιο παρὰ μνηστῇς ἀλόχοισι, / κουράων δ' ἑτέρωθεν ἐναντίοι ἔνδοθεν αὐλῆς / δώδεκ' ἔσαν τέγεοι θάλαμοι ξεστοῖο λίθοιο / πλησίον ἀλλήλων δεδμημένοι, ἔνθα δὲ γαμβροὶ / κοιμῶντο Πριάμοιο παρ' αἰδοίῃς ἀλόχοισιν·
> | **6.242–250**

| Phrase | Licences |
|---|---|
| `δόμον περικαλλέα` | A palace-complex that is *very fine* — dominant, not a modest megaron |
| `ξεστῇς αἰθούσῃσι τετυγμένον` | Built with **polished / dressed colonnades or porticoes** (`αἴθουσαι` = open colonnaded halls; `ξεστός` = worked smooth). Draw dressed stone, not rough rubble |
| `πεντήκοντ' … θάλαμοι ξεστοῖο λίθοιο` | **Fifty bedchambers of dressed stone**, built close together — a large, multi-chambered complex, not a single hall |
| `πλησίον ἀλλήλων δεδμημένοι` | Chambers **adjacent / packed**, not free-standing villas scattered on a lawn |
| sons with wives on one side; daughters' husbands across the courtyard | An **αὐλή** (courtyard) organises the plan: fifty rooms one side, twelve the other |
| `δώδεκ' ἔσαν τέγεοι θάλαμοι` | **Twelve roofed chambers** across the court — `τέγεοι` explicitly says they have roofs |
| `ξεστοῖο λίθοιο` (twice) | Stone is **worked**, not fieldstone random rubble |

**Also:** Priam drives out through a `πρόθυρον` and a **loud-echoing portico** —
`ἐκ δ' ἔλασε προθύροιο καὶ αἰθούσης ἐριδούπου` (**24.323**). Draw a deep porch that can
ring with sound; vehicles pass through it.

**NOT in the text:** column order (Doric etc.), fluting, pediment, number of storeys of
the palace as a whole, roof pitch, courtyard dimensions, wall plaster, painted decoration,
throne room.

### 1.3 Hector's and Paris's houses on the acropolis

> Ἕκτωρ δὲ πρὸς δώματ' Ἀλεξάνδροιο βεβήκει / καλά, τά ῥ' αὐτὸς ἔτευξε σὺν ἀνδράσιν οἳ τότ' ἄριστοι / ἦσαν ἐνὶ Τροίῃ ἐριβώλακι τέκτονες ἄνδρες, / οἵ οἱ ἐποίησαν θάλαμον καὶ δῶμα καὶ αὐλὴν / ἐγγύθι τε Πριάμοιο καὶ Ἕκτορος ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ.
> | **6.313–317**

| Phrase | Licences |
|---|---|
| `δώματ' … καλά` | Paris's house is **fine**, purpose-built |
| built by the best **τέκτονες** in Troy | Skilled carpentry/masonry; not crude vernacular |
| `θάλαμον καὶ δῶμα καὶ αὐλήν` | Three-part house: **bedchamber + main house + courtyard** |
| `ἐγγύθι τε Πριάμοιο καὶ Ἕκτορος` | Paris, Priam, and Hector live **near one another** |
| `ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ` | All three on the **upper city / acropolis** — high ground inside the walls |

Hector reaches `δόμους εὖ ναιετάοντας` — "well-inhabited houses" (**6.370**). The phrase
marks a lived-in, prosperous house, not a ruin.

Helen’s chamber is `ὑψόροφον` — **high-roofed** (**3.423**). Licences a tall interior
ceiling or a high upper storey in elite housing; does not fix pitch.

**NOT in the text:** that Paris’s house is a tower-house; that Hector’s is next to the
Scaean Gate; floor plans; storey counts (inferred only from “high-roofed”).

### 1.4 Temple of Athena in the upper city

> Αἳ δ' ὅτε νηὸν ἵκανον Ἀθήνης ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ, / τῇσι θύρας ὤϊξε Θεανὼ καλλιπάρῃος
> | **6.297–298** (cf. the summons to the same temple at **6.88**: `νηὸν Ἀθηναίης … ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ`)

| Phrase | Licences |
|---|---|
| `νηόν` | A **temple / shrine building**, not an open-air altar alone |
| `ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ` | On the **acropolis**, same high zone as the royal houses |
| `θύρας ὤϊξε` | It has **doors** a priestess can open — enclosed, with a doorway |

Theano is Athena’s priestess by Trojan appointment (**6.300**). Women of the city process
to it with a peplos offering (**6.286–296**).

**NOT in the text:** classical Greek temple form (peristyle, pediment, fluted columns),
cella plan, cult-statue description beyond the peplos being laid on Athena’s knees
(6.303 — cult image with lap, not architecture).

### 1.5 Apollo’s shrine in Pergamos

> Περγάμῳ εἰν ἱερῇ, ὅθι οἱ νηός γε τέτυκτο.
> | **5.446**

Apollo has a **νηός** (temple/shrine) built in **holy Pergamos**. He sits on high Pergamos
(`Περγάμῳ ἄκρῃ`, **5.460**); watches from Pergamos (**4.508**, **7.21**); Paris comes down
from the top of Pergamos (`κατὰ Περγάμου ἄκρης`, **6.512**); Cassandra goes up into
Pergamos and sees Priam (**24.700**).

**Licences:** the citadel summit is sacred high ground with at least Apollo’s shrine and
(separately) Athena’s temple; it is high enough to survey the plain and the ships.
**Does not licence:** classical temple architecture; a multi-shrine skyline of pediments.

### 1.6 The agora at Priam’s doors

> οἳ δ' ἀγορὰς ἀγόρευον ἐπὶ Πριάμοιο θύρῃσι / πάντες ὁμηγερέες ἠμὲν νέοι ἠδὲ γέροντες·
> | **2.788–789**

> Τρώων αὖτ' ἀγορὴ γένετ' Ἰλίου ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ / δεινὴ τετρηχυῖα, παρὰ Πριάμοιο θύρῃσι·
> | **7.345–346**

| Phrase | Licences |
|---|---|
| `ἐπὶ Πριάμοιο θύρῃσι` / `παρὰ Πριάμοιο θύρῃσι` | Public assembly **at / beside Priam’s doors** — open space hard against the palace front |
| `Ἰλίου ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ` | On the **acropolis**, not in a lower-town market outside the walls |
| young and old together | A civic open space large enough for the whole people |

**NOT in the text:** colonnaded stoa, paved forum, shops, a separate commercial agora.

### 1.7 The great tower and the Scaean Gate

The gate and the tower function as one complex for the poem’s civilian scenes.

> αἶψα δ' ἔπειθ' ἵκανον ὅθι Σκαιαὶ πύλαι ἦσαν. … ἥατο δημογέροντες ἐπὶ Σκαιῇσι πύλῃσι, … τοῖοι ἄρα Τρώων ἡγήτορες ἧντ' ἐπὶ πύργῳ.
> | **3.145–153**

Elders sit **at the Scaean gates** and are treated as sitting **on the tower** — same place.
Helen goes **up onto the tower** (`ἐπὶ πύργον`, **3.154**); Aphrodite finds her
`πύργῳ ἐφ' ὑψηλῷ` — **on a high tower** (**3.384**).

> ἀλλ' ἐπὶ πύργον ἔβη μέγαν Ἰλίου
> | **6.386** — Andromache goes to the **great tower of Ilios**

> πύργῳ ἐφεστήκει γοόωσά τε μυρομένη τε.
> | **6.373**

> Ἰλίου προπάροιθε πυλάων τε Σκαιάων.
> | **22.6** — Hector waits **in front of Ilios and the Scaean gates**

> Ἑστήκει δ' ὃ γέρων Πρίαμος θείου ἐπὶ πύργου, … ὀτρύνων παρὰ τεῖχος ἀγακλειτοὺς πυλαωρούς· / πεπταμένας ἐν χερσὶ πύλας ἔχετ' … / αὖτις ἐπανθέμεναι σανίδας πυκινῶς ἀραρυίας·
> | **21.526–535**

| Phrase | Licences |
|---|---|
| `πύργον … μέγαν Ἰλίου` | One tower is **the** great tower of the city — skyline dominant |
| `πύργῳ ἐφ' ὑψηλῷ` | It is **high** enough for a crowd of Trojan women and a view of the plain |
| gate + tower + plain side | The complex **faces the battlefield**; chariots go out through it to the plain (`διὰ Σκαιῶν πεδίον δ'`, **3.263**; `Σκαιάς, τῇ ἄρ' ἔμελλε διεξίμεναι πεδίον δέ`, **6.393**) |
| `πυλαωρούς` | **Gatekeepers** at the gates |
| `σανίδας πυκινῶς ἀραρυίας` | **Thick-fitted wooden door-planks** that close the opening |
| `ὑψηλαί τε πύλαι σανίδες τ' ἐπὶ τῇς ἀραρυῖαι / μακραὶ ἐΰξεστοι ἐζευγμέναι` (**18.275–276**) | Gates are **high**; door-leaves are **long, well-smoothed, barred** |

The oak stands with the Scaean Gate as one formula: `Σκαιάς τε πύλας καὶ φηγόν` (**6.237**,
**9.354**, **11.170**). Licences a large tree **outside** the wall by the main gate — not
inside the citadel.

**NOT in the text:** which excavated gate is the Scaean (see `RESEARCH-CITADEL.md` §5 —
speculative; no coordinate); merlons; drawbridge; iron hinges; sculpted gate reliefs.

### 1.8 Walls Hector runs; wagon-road; two springs

> τείχεος αἰὲν ὑπ' ἐκ κατ' ἀμαξιτὸν ἐσσεύοντο, / κρουνὼ δ' ἵκανον καλλιρρόω· ἔνθα δὲ πηγαὶ / δοιαὶ ἀναΐσσουσι Σκαμάνδρου δινήεντος. … ἔνθα δ' ἐπ' αὐτάων πλυνοὶ εὐρέες ἐγγὺς ἔασι / καλοὶ λαΐνεοι
> | **22.146–154**

| Phrase | Licences |
|---|---|
| running always **under the wall** out along the **wagon-road** (`ἀμαξιτόν`) | A **cart-road** under the exterior of the circuit — wide enough for wheeled traffic, hugging the wall on the chase circuit |
| twin springs of the Scamander | Two springs outside the wall on that circuit |
| `πλυνοὶ … καλοὶ λαΐνεοι` | **Wide, fine stone washing-troughs** at the springs — worked stone street-furniture outside the city |

Hector tries for the Dardanian gates to get under the **well-built towers** so his people
can shoot over him:

> ὁσσάκι δ' ὁρμήσειε πυλάων Δαρδανιάων / ἀντίον ἀΐξασθαι ἐϋδμήτους ὑπὸ πύργους
> | **22.194–195**

Licences: **more than one gate** (Scaean and Dardanian); towers over gates that project
enough for defenders to shoot down; towers described as `ἐϋδμήτους` (well-built).

Trojans lean on **fine parapets** as Achilles approaches:

> κεκλιμένοι καλῇσιν ἐπάλξεσιν
> | **22.3**

Licences a **parapet / battlement walk** on the wall-top that people can lean on. (The
Achaean wall has the same vocabulary in Book 12; for Troy the key line is 22.3.)

They thrice circle **Priam’s city** (`Πριάμοιο πόλιν πέρι`, **22.165**). Licences a circuit
that can be run around — a free-standing citadel with open ground outside, not a city
buried in continuous suburbs tight to the wall all the way round (though a lower town may
exist; the chase circuit needs clear running ground on the plain side).

**NOT in the text:** hot-spring steam as a *required* visual if the artist cannot place a
thermal spring honestly (hydrochemistry finds no true hot pair at Hisarlık — see
`TROAD-SOURCES.md`); exact spring coordinates; that the wagon-road is paved.

### 1.9 Epithets of Ilios / Troy — what each licences

Corpus sweeps (all 24 books). Counts are for the forms named.

#### εὐρυάγυια — “wide-streeted”

| Line | Greek (context) |
|---|---|
| 2.12, 2.29, 2.66 | `πόλιν εὐρυάγυιαν` (Troy, dream-speech) |
| 2.141, 2.329 | `Τροίην` / `πόλιν … εὐρυάγυιαν` |
| 9.28 | `Τροίην … εὐρυάγυιαν` |
| 14.88 | `Τρώων πόλιν εὐρυάγυιαν` |
| (4.52 = Mycenae, not Troy) | — |

**Licences:** streets that read as **broad** — not a cramped alley maze as the first
impression. At least the main routes. **Does not licence:** a Hippodamian grid, paved
boulevards, colonnaded streets.

Related: Hector goes `ἐϋκτιμένας κατ' ἀγυιάς` — **well-built streets** (**6.391**).
Heracles once “widowed the streets” of Ilios (`χήρωσε δ' ἀγυιάς`, **5.642**).

#### εὐτείχεος / εὐτείχεον — “well-walled”

1.129, 2.113, 2.288, 5.716, 8.241, 9.20; cf. `πόλιν εὐτείχεα` 16.57.

**Licences:** walls that look **formidable and finished** — the dominant visual fact of the
city from outside.

#### αἰπεινή — “steep / sheer”

`Ἰλίου αἰπεινῆς` 9.419, 9.686, 15.215; `Ἴλιος αἰπεινή` 13.773; `Ἴλιον αἰπεινήν` 15.558,
17.328; related `αἰπεινὸν πτολίεθρον` 15.257.

**Licences:** a city that **rises steeply** — hillside massing, walls climbing a spur, not
a flat pancake town on a plain. Combine with the real Hisarlık spur (§4).

#### ἠνεμόεσσα — “windy”

`Ἴλιον ἠνεμόεσσαν` 3.305, 8.499, 12.115, 13.724, 18.174, 23.64, 23.297.

**Licences:** a site **exposed to wind** — open ridge/spur position; banners, dust, or
wind-bent vegetation are fair; still air and heavy fog as a default climate mood are not.

#### εὔπυργος — “fair-towered”

`Τροίην εὔπυργον` **7.71**.

**Licences:** towers as a **defining, beautiful** part of the skyline — not a bare curtain
wall alone.

#### ὑψίπυλος — “high-gated”

`ὑψίπυλον Τροίην` **16.698**, **21.544**.

**Licences:** **tall gate openings** / high gate architecture — gates that read as high in
the wall face.

#### ἐϋδμητος / well-built (walls, towers, city)

`τεῖχος ἐϋδμήτοιο πόληος` **21.516**; `ἐϋδμήτους ὑπὸ πύργους` **22.195**; Apollo stands
`ἐϋδμήτου ἐπὶ πύργου` **16.700**.

**Licences:** craftsmanship visible in the masonry — regular coursing, fitted stone.

#### θεοδμήτων … πύργων — “god-built towers”

`θεοδμήτων ἐπὶ πύργων` **8.519**.

**Licences:** towers of **exceptional, almost superhuman quality** (Poseidon built the wall
for Laomedon in the tradition the poem knows — 7.452–453, 21.446–447). Visual: walls that
look beyond ordinary village work. **Does not licence:** glowing magic stone or non-human
scale that breaks the archaeological massing.

#### Ἴλιος ἱρή — “holy Ilios”

Formulaic (e.g. 4.416, 5.648, 6.96, 7.82, 21.515, 24.143). Marks the city as sacred in
epithet; does not add architectural form.

#### Ἴλιος ὀφρυόεσσα — “beetling / brow-like Ilios”

`Ἴλιος ὀφρυόεσσα` **22.411**.

**Licences:** a **brow / cliff-edge** silhouette — the city sits on a brow overlooking the
plain, like a forehead over eyes. Strong for oblique views from the plain.

#### ἐριβώλαξ — “deep-soiled” (of the land of Troy)

`Τροίῃ ἐριβώλακι` **6.315**. The *land* is fertile; the city sits in rich country. Softens
a pure desert-rock palette in the surrounding fields.

### 2.1 Fortification wall — socle, batter, thickness, height

#### Plan massing (cross-ref only)

Circuit of the Late Troy VI citadel wall ≈ **540–550 m**; ≈ **330 m** still preserved
(Tolman & Scoggin 1903, §10; standard modern figure ~550 m). Enclosed citadel area
≈ **2 ha** (20,000 m²). Overall acropolis footprint on the order of **~220 × 180 m**
(see `RESEARCH-CITADEL.md`). North and northwest circuit destroyed; Dörpfeld refused even
to dash it in on Tafel V (Dörpfeld 1902, 2:650).

#### Masonry

- **Material:** local **limestone**, cut into rectangular face-blocks (ashlar faces), not
  always isodomic (courses vary in height). Joints staggered for strength (Rutter, Lesson
  23, after Blegen).
- **Build type:** solid mass of fitted blocks making contact through the wall’s thickness —
  **not** two megalithic skins with rubble core (that is Mycenaean Cyclopean). Distinct
  from both Cyclopean and from classic Minoan ashlar-skin technique (Rutter, Lesson 23).
- **Quality varies by sector:** West wall poorer / smaller stones; East wall better; South
  wall and towers finest, with blocks up to ~1.50 m long × ~0.30 m high, closely joined
  without rubble fill (Tolman & Scoggin 1903, §§11, 14–15).

#### Thickness

- Stone socle / lower wall: **~4.5–5 m** thick on the East wall (Tolman: 4.60–5 m; UNESCO
  ICOMOS 1998: “more than 4 m”; common modern summary 4–5 m).
- Late replacement upper stone wall (where mudbrick was replaced): **~1.80–2 m** thick
  (Tolman §14; Rutter Lesson 23: ~2 m).

#### Batter / slope (the characteristic profile)

Exterior face is **strongly battered** (slopes inward as it rises); interior face is
**vertical** (Rutter, Lesson 23; Dörpfeld via Tolman).

Published scarp ratios (horizontal setback per metre of height), Tolman & Scoggin 1903:

| Sector | Scarp (m setback / m height) | ≈ angle from vertical | ≈ angle from horizontal |
|---|---|---|---|
| West wall | 0.40 | ~22° | ~68° |
| East wall | 0.37 | ~20° | ~70° |
| South wall | 0.23 | ~13° | ~77° |

**Artist rule:** the outside of the citadel wall is a **sloping stone face**, not a
vertical cliff of ashlar. The slope is gentle-to-moderate, closest to Hittite practice
(comparison in §3), not a pyramid. Towers and gates break the slope with more nearly
vertical faces of finer ashlar (Tolman fig. description of Tower VI h vs curtain).

#### Vertical offsets / “sawtooth” jogs

The wall is a **polygon of short straight segments**, each side about **7–10 m** long,
meeting at shallow **advancing angles** (ressaults). Projection of the jog is small:
typically **0.10–0.15 m**, occasionally **0.30 m** (Tolman & Scoggin 1903, §12). Dörpfeld
and Blegen treat these as a structural/decorative system; Rutter (Lesson 23) notes they
appear from Early Troy VI and look **decorative rather than strictly functional**, and
that the same offset habit appears on some interior buildings (e.g. House VI M).

**Why they are there (published views):**

1. Construction in **sections** over time (Late VI wall rebuilt sector by sector —
   Rutter’s Sections 1–6 phasing).
2. **Timber-frame alignment:** some modern site presentations (Troia Project interpretive
   material, e.g. Madain Project summary of the East wall) hold that the offsets continued
   the lines of corners of a **timber-framed mudbrick superstructure** — i.e. the stone
   jogs visually “found” the upper frame. Treat as a **plausible reconstruction inference**,
   not a fully preserved upper storey.
3. Analogy with Mycenaean and Egyptian offset walls (Tolman §12).

**Artist rule:** do not draw a smooth circular curtain. Draw a **faceted, slightly
sawtoothed** limestone ring.

#### Preserved height vs estimated original height

| Element | Preserved (stone) | Estimated original |
|---|---|---|
| East wall stone socle | **~4–6 m** (Tolman: lower wall ~6 m; frontispiece 4–5 m) | Stone portion to **~6 m**, plus mudbrick |
| Stone portion “in places” | up to **~9 m** claimed in some Blegen-based summaries (Rutter: “some nine meters high in places”) | Contested range — see below |
| Superstructure (mudbrick, later thin stone) | mostly gone; thin upper stone preserved to **~2 m** in best spots (Tolman §14) | **+3 to +4 m** of mudbrick commonly restored (Troia Project / site signage tradition; Korfmann & Mannsperger 1998 summary: height over 6 m stone + mudbrick superstructure) |
| **Working total height for art** | — | **~9–10 m** for the main curtain is the defensible central figure; some stretches of pure stone may have stood higher before loss |

**Range to name honestly:**

- **UNESCO ICOMOS 1998:** East wall “originally stood to a height of **6 m**” and was more
  than 4 m thick; NE bastion originally **9 m**, **7 m** survive.
- **Tolman/Dörpfeld:** East lower wall ~**6 m** stone + upper wall (brick then stone).
- **Rutter after Blegen:** stone “some **nine** meters high in places” + mudbrick above.
- **Popular Troia Project figures:** wall **4–5 m** thick, **>6 m** stone, **+3–4 m**
  mudbrick → **~9–10 m** total.

**Do not draw** 20 m medieval curtains. **Do draw** a wall a modern person finds
intimidating but not mythical — roughly three storeys of sloping stone plus a vertical
mudbrick breastwork.

#### Mudbrick superstructure

**Evidence it existed:**

- Remains of **clay brick in Tower VI g** (Northeast Tower) show the superstructure was
  originally mudbrick (Tolman & Scoggin 1903, §14; Rutter Lesson 23: mudbrick still *in
  situ* in VI g when examined).
- In late phases of Troy VI, the mudbrick upper work was **replaced** in most sections by
  a **thinner vertical stone wall** ~2 m thick, with a **parapet / fighting platform
  2–3 m wide** behind it, the platform ~2 m above interior ground level (Rutter, Lesson 23,
  Section 3 evidence). Whether the parapet was roofed is **unknown**.

**Timber:** Tolman (§9) records wood for tower platforms, beams in some walls, pillars, and
doors. A timber-laced mudbrick breastwork is a reasonable reconstruction; full proof of a
continuous timber frame along the whole circuit is not preserved.

**Artist rule:** top of the wall = **vertical band of mudbrick** (tan/brown) or late thin
stone, set back slightly on the battered limestone socle; defenders’ heads and spears can
show above a parapet. No stone machicolations.

### 2.2 Towers

Dimensions after Tolman & Scoggin 1903 (§§ and Dörpfeld’s labels); heights after UNESCO
ICOMOS 1998 and site syntheses.

| Tower | Plan | Projection | Notes for elevation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VI g** (NE, “most stately”) | **18 m** broad × **8 m** deep | projects **8 m** | Stone substructure originally **~9 m** high, **~7 m** survive; mudbrick superstructure above, form unknown; contains a deep well/cistern; accessible also from outside in some phases |
| **VI h** (East) | **>11 m** broad; front wall **~3 m** thick | projects **8 m** | Finer ashlar than adjacent curtain; two-storey reconstruction often shown (wooden floor); access from wall-walk level in modern reconstructions |
| **VI i** (South, by South Gate) | front wall **4.40 m** broad; sides **2.20 m** | gate-flanking | Associated with monolithic **stelae** standing before the gate (cultic?); possible shrine traces inside |

**Upper storeys:** not preserved as standing architecture. Inferences:

- VI h is widely reconstructed as **two storeys** with a wooden intermediate floor (Troia
  Project / interpretive tradition; Madain Project summary).
- Fighting platforms on towers are required by the wall’s military logic and by Homer’s
  people standing `ἐπὶ πύργῳ`, but the **roof form of towers is not known** (flat fighting
  deck vs light canopy).

**Artist rule:** towers **break out** from the battered curtain as taller, more vertical
ashlar masses; at least one (NE) is a true skyline marker; mudbrick tops them as on the
curtain. Do not invent machicolated tops or conical caps.

### 2.3 Gates

| Gate | Opening width | Form notes |
|---|---|---|
| **VI S** East | passage **~2 m**; door opening **~1.80 m** | Overlapping walls create a **cul-de-sac** approach; assailant exposed on two sides (Tolman §16; Mycenae/Tiryns analogy for overlap) |
| **VI T** South | **3.20–3.35 m** | Principal gate; paved with stone; drain canal **0.50 m** deep, **0.30–0.40 m** wide under pavement; flanked by South Tower VI i; stelae before the front |
| **VI U** West | opening **~2.50 m** | Opens toward the plateau / lower town side |

**Doors:** stone thresholds and openings preserved in plan; **leaves** are not preserved.
Homer’s `σανίδες` (wooden planks) are the right reconstruction for the movable doors
(§1.7). Gate towers / flanking: South Gate + VI i is the clearest gate-tower pair.

**Artist rule:** deep, shadowed gate passages in thick walls; wooden doors; South Gate the
widest and most ceremonial; no triumphal arches, no classical pediments over gates.

### 2.4 Houses of Troy VI (citadel)

**Plan types:** free-standing large houses on **concentric terraces** rising toward the
centre; short sides of buildings often radial to the circuit (Rutter, Lesson 23; Blegen).
Not party-wall apartment blocks (that is more Troy V / VIIa crowding).

Named types:

- Megaron-like: Houses **VI A, VI B, VI C**.
- Pillar House: large pillars tapering upward, likely supporting a second storey;
  entrance in the long wall.
- **VI F:** beam-slots for half-timbering; stone column bases; evidence of **interior stair**
  to a second storey; multi-phase.
- **VI M:** L-shaped, open court between wings; long carefully built retaining wall with
  sloping face and vertical offsets (~27 m long) — shows that even **house terraces** can
  look like small fortification faces from below.
- **VI E, VI G,** etc.: large rectangular elite houses.

**Storeys:** several houses have **evidence for two storeys** (stairs, pillar supports,
beam arrangements). Rutter: broad ground-floor spans imply **light upper storeys (wood
rather than heavy mudbrick)**; storage on ground floor, living above — a pattern familiar
in later Anatolian vernacular.

**Wall construction:** stone socles / lower walls of worked limestone; mudbrick and timber
above (half-timbering attested in beam-slots, House VI F). Exterior faces of elite houses
can mirror fortification finish (smooth ashlar, offsets).

**Roofs:** Dörpfeld/Tolman state that **clay was used for horizontal roofs** of houses
(Tolman & Scoggin 1903, §9). That is the primary published claim for roof form:
**flat (or very low-pitch) clay roofs**, not tiled gables. No Troy VI roof tiles of a
classical kind are the basis of reconstruction. Timber beams carried the roof.

**Terracing implication:** from outside and from below, the citadel reads as **steps of
roofs and retaining walls** rising to a central high point — not a single plateau of equal
roofs. The Hellenistic/Roman shaving of the summit destroyed the palace centre; the royal
focus is **inferred at the apex**, not preserved (Rutter; Rose 2014).

**Artist rule:** large freestanding houses, some two-storeyed, flat clay roofs, stone below
and mudbrick/timber above, packed on rising terraces inside a faceted wall. Streets between
them can be relatively wide for a Bronze Age citadel (poem’s εὐρυάγυια is compatible).

### 2.5 Lower town

**What is solid:**

- A **rock-cut ditch** outside the citadel, traced by the Troia Project (magnetometry and
  excavation). Facts citable in prose: on the order of **hundreds of metres** traced
  (figures in the literature range; `RESEARCH-CITADEL.md` records ~**700 m** traced, **≤3 m**
  wide, **~1.5 m** deep, Troy VI/VII), with **gaps / “bridges”** for entries, and an
  **inner shallower cutting** interpreted as a palisade/stockade line (Blindow–Hübner–Jansen
  and Korfmann project reports; Rutter Lesson 23; Aramco World summary of Korfmann’s
  ditch-and-fence reading).
- House remains **west of the citadel** and scattered lower-town architecture; density is
  **debated**.

**What is contested:**

- Korfmann’s **fortified lower city** of ~**15–35 ha** with a substantial outer wall vs
  Kolb’s critique that the lower-city wall and dense settlement are overstated (Kolb, “Troy
  VI: A Trading Center and Commercial City?” *AJA* 108 [2004]: 577–614).
- Population estimates (often 5,000–10,000 in popular and some scholarly summaries) are
  **model-dependent**, not a measured census.

**Artist rule for a Homer-first picture:**

- The **citadel** is the visual subject: walls, towers, terraced houses, temples on the
  brow.
- A **lower settlement** may spread on the plateau south and west — lower, less monumental,
  mudbrick and timber, not a second ashlar ring unless labelled schematic.
- The **ditch** can appear as a dark linear cut in the rock on the approaches; a wooden
  palisade is a defensible light reconstruction just inside it.
- Do not draw a continuous 20 m ashlar city wall around 30 ha as if it were excavated fact.

### 2.6 Materials and COLOUR

| Material | What it is at Hisarlık | Colour for art |
|---|---|---|
| **Limestone** (walls, house socles, troughs, paving) | Local Tertiary limestone of the Troad ridge; cut as ashlar on VI walls | **Cream, buff, pale grey, warm white** — not Carrara white, not black basalt. Fresh cuts lighter; weathered faces slightly greyer and lichen-soft. Joints thin and dark |
| **Mudbrick** | Sun-dried clay brick on stone socles; superstructure of walls and houses | **Tan, ochre, light brown** when dry; darker when wet or new. Vertical upper wall band |
| **Timber** | Beams, pillars, doors, tower floors, roof structure (Tolman §9) | **Brown wood** — doors (`σανίδες`), posts, roof edges; not painted polychrome without evidence |
| **Clay roof surfaces** | Horizontal clay roofs (Tolman §9) | **Dull earth brown / grey-brown** flat planes; slight fall for drainage is artist’s licence |
| **Plaster / paint** | Not established as a general exterior finish for Troy VI fortifications. Interior plaster possible in houses but poorly preserved for reconstruction of colour schemes | Do **not** invent bright painted wall frescoes on the citadel exterior. Thera-style frescoes are analogy (§3), not Troy evidence |
| **Lime** | Lime found in pithoi in Tower VI h; lime cement on a steep ascent near VI M; Dörpfeld doubted lime was a general building binder (Tolman §9) | Occasional pale mortar smear, not whitewashed fortress |

**Light:** NW Anatolian summer light is hard and clear; limestone bleaches; mudbrick goes
matte. Winter and storm light greys the plain. “Windy Ilios” suggests moving cloud shadow
and dust off the plain.

## 3. Comparative evidence for what is not preserved

**Rule:** everything in this section is **ANALOGY**. It fills blanks; it does not upgrade
them to Troy VI facts. Rank is from most to least defensible for a careful reconstruction.

### 3.1 Rank 1 — West Anatolian / Hittite fortification practice (strong analogy)

**Hattusa** (Boğazköy): monumental stone-socle walls with **mudbrick superstructures**,
**battered faces**, towers, and complex gates. The **Yerkapı** section is the classic
published model of a sloping stone glacis/wall with upper brickwork and a ceremonial
passage — the closest large-scale Anatolian parallel for “how a Late Bronze Age capital’s
wall stands up.”

**Why defensible for Troy:** Troy VI’s wall profile (battered exterior, vertical interior,
mudbrick top, towers, overlapping gates) sits in the **Anatolian** military tradition more
than in the Mycenaean Cyclopean one (Rutter Lesson 23 stresses the technical difference
from Mycenae/Tiryns). Rose 2014 and the Troia Project consistently frame LBA Troy as an
Anatolian centre with Aegean contacts.

**Use for:** wall cross-section, mudbrick breastwork, tower massing, gate tunnels, scale of
monumental ashlar.

**Do not import:** Hittite orthostat relief programs, sphinx gates as Trojan fact, or
Hattusa’s specific ground plan.

### 3.2 Rank 2 — Other West Anatolian LBA sites (moderate)

Limited published elevation for peers (e.g. coastal West Anatolian citadels). Useful for
**house: stone socle + mudbrick + flat roof** as a regional vernacular. Weaker than Hattusa
for fortification spectacle because fewer complete publications match Troy’s wall quality.

### 3.3 Rank 3 — Mycenaean citadels (selective analogy)

**Mycenae, Tiryns, Gla:** multi-gate systems, overlapping approaches, towers by gates,
elevated citadels. Tolman already compared Troy’s East Gate cul-de-sac to Mycenae/Tiryns.

**Limits:** Cyclopean rubble-core masonry ≠ Troy’s solid ashlar; Mycenaean elevations
often steeper and more “cliff wall.” Use for **gate tactics and citadel psychology**, not
for copying Tiryns’ wall face onto Hisarlık.

### 3.4 Rank 4 — Thera / Akrotiri house frescoes (elevation & openings)

Akrotiri frescoes show **multi-storey houses**, windows, balconies, flat roofs, and
coloured wall faces in an LBA Aegean town.

**Use for:** *possibility* of upper-storey windows, roof terraces, and lived-in house
elevation when Troy’s own walls preserve only ground plans.

**Limits:** Thera is a Cycladic town under strong Minoan influence; Troy’s elite houses
are freestanding megaron-related halls on terraces, not a continuous Theran streetscape.
**Windows at Troy are not attested.** If drawn, keep them few, small, and high in mudbrick
storeys — and treat as licence.

### 3.5 Rank 5 — Terracotta house models (weak–moderate for roofs)

Aegean and Near Eastern terracotta house models (Minoan and later) often show **flat or
low roofs**, door placement, and sometimes upper storeys.

**Use for:** supporting flat-roof reconstruction already suggested by Dörpfeld’s clay
roofs claim.

**Limits:** models are schematic and rarely Trojan; do not copy a specific model’s
decoration as Troy VI fact.

### 3.6 Rank 6 — Later Anatolian vernacular (weak, last resort)

Traditional timber-upper / stone-lower houses of western Turkey (invoked by Rutter for
the ground-storage / upper-living pattern). Useful only for **lived-in logic**, not for
LBA dateable form.

### 3.7 Explicit non-sources for form

- Classical Greek temples and stoas (wrong period and style for LBA Troy).
- Roman Ilion (Troy VIII–IX): Odeon, Athena temple precinct, baths — **do not** bleed into
  a Bronze Age view except as a separate “later city” plate.
- Medieval castles, Crusader crenellations, Islamic domes.

## 4. The setting

Enough background that a picture’s horizon and ground plane are right. Details and
citations for paleogeography live in `RESEARCH-PALEOGEOGRAPHY.md` and `TROAD-SOURCES.md`;
this is the artist’s extract.

### 4.1 The mound and the spur

- **Hisarlık** is a low **spur** at the west end of a limestone ridge between the Scamander
  (Karamenderes) and Simoeis (Dümrek) valleys — not an isolated sugarloaf volcano.
- Citadel height today is on the order of **~30–40 m above sea level** for the mound top
  (common survey figures cluster near **~36 m** asl for the plateau/mound; local relief
  above the plain is much less than a mountain). The ridge **rises inland** to the east
  (on the order of **~50–60 m** within ~1–2 km — enough that the citadel is the brow of a
  higher eastern upland, matching `ὀφρυόεσσα` and `αἰπεινή`).
- North face of the mound is steep (Schliemann’s great trench; lost north wall). South and
  east open onto the plateau of the lower town.

### 4.2 The plain

- **West and south:** the Trojan / Scamandrian plain — the poem’s battlefield. In the Late
  Bronze Age the usable plain lay **south and west** of the city more than on the modern
  northern flats (Kraft, Kayan & Erol, *Science* 209 [1980]: 776–82).
- Ground: alluvial, fertile (`ἐριβώλαξ`), seasonally wet; not desert sand; not alpine
  meadow.

### 4.3 Water and the shore

- **Scamander** (Karamenderes) and **Simoeis** (Dümrek Su) frame the plain.
- **Bronze Age shoreline:** not at the modern coast (~6 km north). A marine embayment once
  ran far inland; by the LBA, progradation and a relative sea-level fall left a **shallow
  lagoon / wetland system behind a sandy barrier**, with the open sea more distant than
  today (Kayan’s work; Kraft et al. 2003). Troy overlooks **marsh, lagoon light, and distant
  water**, not a deep harbour at the walls.
- **Do not draw** a medieval seaport quay under the Scaean Gate.

### 4.4 Horizon

| Direction | Feature | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SE–S | **Mount Ida** (Kaz Dağı) | Poem’s many-fountained mountain; Zeus’s seat; real massif on the southern horizon |
| NW | **Samothrace** (Fengari) | Visible in clear weather; Poseidon’s lookout in the poem (13.11–14) — optical fact |
| W–SW | Aegean / island hints | Tenedos (Bozcaada), Imbros farther; often haze |
| N | Dardanelles / Hellespont direction | Water-glint possible; not a fjord at the city’s feet |

### 4.5 Light, wind, vegetation

- **Prevailing wind:** northerlies / etesian pattern over the Dardanelles region —
  consistent with the epithet **ἠνεμόεσσα**. Draw wind as a climate fact, not a one-off
  gust.
- **Vegetation:** Mediterranean–Anatolian: oak (the poem’s φηγός by the Scaean Gate), fig
  (ἐρινεός), scrub on the ridge, cultivated plain, reeds/wetland plants toward the lagoon.
  Not northern European forest; not barren rock only.
- **Season default for “the war” pictures:** dry-season dust and hard light are fair;
  deep snow is wrong for the main campaigning image.

2. **Wall height:** restorations range from modest ~6 m stone walls to ~9–10 m total with

3. **Roofscape:** flat clay roofs vs occasional invented gables or Mediterranean tiles.
4. **Gate identity:** tourist art often labels South Gate “Scaean”; scholarship does not
   settle that (`RESEARCH-CITADEL.md` §5).

## 6. An artist’s brief

### 6.1 ~400 words — Homer’s Troy as it should look

Troy sits on a low limestone brow where a ridge ends above the Scamandrian plain. From
the plain the city is steep and windy: a faceted ring of **pale cream-buff ashlar** walls,
each face sloping inward as it rises, broken every few metres by shallow vertical jogs.
The stone stands roughly **two human storeys and more**; on top runs a **vertical band of
mudbrick**, tan against the stone, with a parapet defenders can lean on. Towers punch
out from the circuit — one northeastern mass especially large — taller, more upright, still
capped in brick. Gates are deep shadowed passages in the thickness of the wall; the main
ones carry **high wooden doors** of fitted planks. Outside the principal plain-facing gate
stand a great **oak** and open ground; a **wagon-road** hugs the wall; farther along, stone
washing-troughs sit by springs.

Inside the ring the ground **rises in terraces**. Large freestanding houses of stone and
mudbrick, some two storeys with light upper walls, pack the terraces under **flat clay
roofs**. Streets between them are broad enough to earn the name wide-streeted. At the top,
near one another, stand the great houses of the royal family: Priam’s complex with
**polished porticoes**, a courtyard, and dozens of dressed-stone chambers; Hector’s and
Paris’s houses close by. An open **agora** spreads before Priam’s doors. On the high
citadel also sit the **shrines** of Athena and Apollo — enclosed sacred buildings with
doors, not classical temples with pediments and fluted columns.

Beyond the citadel a lower settlement may spill onto the southern and western plateau,
lower and browner, ditch-cut at its edge, never overpowering the ashlar crown. The plain
below is fertile and dusty, veined by the Scamander and Simoeis, with wetland and lagoon
light toward a distant sea. On the horizon: **Ida** to the south-east, and in clear air
**Samothrace** far north-west. Wind moves over the ridge. This is a lived Late Bronze Age
Anatolian capital as the poem imagines it standing — steep, well-walled, fair-towered,
holy, and windy — not a classical marble city and not a medieval castle.

### 6.2 DO NOT DRAW

Anachronisms generic image models invent and that must be excluded:

1. **Greek temples** with Doric/Ionic fluted columns, pediments, metopes, or peristyles.
2. **Roman arches**, vaults, domes, aqueducts, or brick-faced concrete.
3. **Classical white marble** cityscape / polished Parian gleam.
4. **Medieval crenellations**, machicolations, drawbridges, portcullises, conical tower
   roofs, keeps.
5. **Islamic or Ottoman domes** and minarets.
6. **Northern European half-timber fantasy** as the primary wall look (timber is real in
   structure; the fortification face is ashlar limestone).
7. **Modern Turkish village** red tile roofs as the default LBA roofscape.
8. **Harbour quays and triremes at the foot of the walls.**
9. **A labelled “Scaean Gate” on a specific excavated gate** as if proven.
10. **Movie branding**, classical cuirass tourist kitsch, or the Trojan Horse as architecture.
11. **Smooth circular curtain walls** without polygonal jogs.
12. **Vertical ashlar cliffs** with no batter on the exterior face.
13. **Bright frescoed exterior fortifications** without labelling them as pure invention.
14. **Snow-capped Alps** behind the city; Ida is a real but regional massif, not the
    Himalaya.
