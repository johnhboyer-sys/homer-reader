# Depictions of Troy: a draughtsman's survey

**Date:** 2026-08-14. **What this is:** a survey of how artists have actually drawn
Troy — the city, the plain, the camp, the siege — across periods and media, aimed at
fixing why our raised-oblique plate of the plain reads as a video-game render rather
than a drawing. Companion to `docs/research/RESEARCH-TROY-APPEARANCE.md` (what the
*Iliad* says Troy looked like — not repeated here) and `docs/TROAD-CARTOGRAPHY.md`
(Pope 1716 as our schematic-plate ancestor, already documented there in depth).

**Copyright posture, per project rule:** study freely, reproduce nothing, trace
nothing. Rights status given for every work by **US** law (pre-1931 publication is
public domain as of 2026). PD works can be studied closely and even referenced;
in-copyright works are analysis-only, exactly as `docs/TROAD-SOURCES.md` treats
Kraft/Kayan/Rose. Every citation below carries a stable URL.

## The survey, by period

### Renaissance and Baroque painting

This tradition draws the *fall* of Troy, not the plain — burning city, the horse,
Aeneas fleeing — and it solves a problem we actually have: **how to render a city at
small scale so it reads as a place and not a shape.**

**Adam Elsheimer, *The Burning of Troy*** (oil on copper, 35.8 × 48.8 cm, Alte
Pinakothek, Munich, c. 1600–1601). PD-US (pre-1931; artist d. 1610).
[Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Adam_Elsheimer_-_The_Burning_of_Troy_-_WGA7505.jpg).
A night scene: the fire itself supplies the value structure. Nothing in the city is
drawn at uniform resolution — buildings nearest the flame edge are silhouette and
glow, buildings further back are suggested by broken roofline against smoke, and the
ships and camp beyond dissolve into a dark middle distance with no drawn detail at
all. The eye goes to the fire and to Aeneas's group; everything else is tone, not
line.

**Giandomenico Tiepolo, *The Building of the Trojan Horse*** (c. 1760, National
Gallery, London, NG3318). PD-US (pre-1931; artist d. 1804).
[National Gallery record](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/giovanni-domenico-tiepolo-the-building-of-the-trojan-horse) ·
[ArtUK](https://artuk.org/discover/artworks/the-building-of-the-trojan-horse-115487).
Troy is present only as a fragment: battlements loom over the scaffolding, with
rooftops and a single spire just visible past the wall-top. The whole city is
implied by one silhouette edge and one accent (the spire) — synecdoche, not a
elevation. All the drawn energy goes into the foreground labourers.

### 18th–19th century book illustration and engraving — our own lineage

**John Harris (engr.), "Troja cum locis pertingentibus," in Pope's *Iliad*, vol. 2**
(London: Bowyer for Lintott, 1716). PD-US.
[Wikimedia Commons, British Library copy](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:THE_ILLIAD_OF_HOMER_(translated_by_POPE)_p2.012_Troia.jpg).
Already the acknowledged ancestor of our schematic plate (`docs/TROAD-CARTOGRAPHY.md`
§"Alexander Pope, 1716"). Worth restating for the *drawing*, not the cartography: it
letters the plain by book and battle-phase rather than drawing troop movement, and —
directly relevant to our 459-hull problem — **it draws the fleet as a single
undifferentiated repeating row of ship glyphs**, not 459 individuated hulls
(`TROAD-CARTOGRAPHY.md:403`). The ships carry the *idea* of the fleet (a line closing
the beach) rather than an inventory of it.

**John Flaxman (des.) / Tommaso Piroli (engr.), *The Iliad of Homer*** (London,
1793), e.g. "Ajax Defending the Greek Ships." PD-US.
[Met Museum record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/337355) ·
[Royal Academy](https://www.royalacademy.org.uk/art-artists/book/the-iliad-of-homer-engraved-from-the-compositions-of-iohn-flaxman-r-a).
A near-total dead end for landscape technique — Flaxman draws pure figure outline on
blank paper, no ground, no distance, no terrain at all, following the vase-painting
convention he was imitating. But the dead end is itself instructive: even ships and a
camp can be reduced to a handful of schematic hull-and-mast marks plus figures, with
the *paper itself* standing for undrawn ground. Zero information is sometimes more
legible than an attempt at ground texture that doesn't commit to a technique.

**Choiseul-Gouffier, *Voyage pittoresque de la Grèce* / *Voyage pittoresque dans
l'empire ottoman... la Troade...*** (Paris, 1782–1822; Troad volume 1809). PD-US.
[Gallica atlas](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5698421z) ·
[Met record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/347535) ·
[archive.org copy](https://archive.org/details/gri_33125008279693).
The first serious topographic-artistic survey of the Troad landscape itself (168
plates, drawn from life by artists including Moreau le Jeune), predating both
Schliemann and Baedeker by three-quarters of a century and directly seeding every
later travellers' view. I could not get plate-by-plate content out of Gallica's
viewer via fetch (403); this is a strong lead for a follow-up pass with direct
image access, not a confirmed technique source — flagged rather than padded.

**Murray, *Handbook for Travellers in Constantinople... and Plain of Troy*** (London,
1871 ed. on). PD-US.
[archive.org](https://archive.org/details/Travel1871MurrayHandbook).
Confirmed to include folding maps and plans of the Troad; Baedeker/Murray's
signature device across the whole series is the labelled skyline panorama (a drawn
horizon strip with peaks and named features keyed by number underneath) — a real,
useful convention for a "what you'd actually see from here" plate — but I could not
confirm the Troy volume uses it (vs. a plan) without pulling the full scan. **Dead
end as currently verified; worth a dedicated follow-up if John wants the panorama
convention specifically.**

### Excavation-era reconstruction (Schliemann, Dörpfeld)

**Schliemann, *Ilios*** (London, 1880) and *Troja* (1884). PD-US. [Ilios context](https://www.rct.uk/collection/1077160/ilios-the-city-and-country-of-the-trojans-the-results-of-researches-and).
Mostly a dead end for landscape drawing: its plates are trench sections and pottery
plates, not perspective views of a living place — terrain appears only as annotated
strata in a vertical cut. Useful for one thing only: it shows terrain-as-evidence,
the opposite mode from terrain-as-picture, and confirms that nobody in this
generation drew Troy as a place — only as a find.

**Dörpfeld, *Troja und Ilion*** (Athens, 1902), 2 vols., 76 plates. PD-US.
[vol. 1](https://archive.org/details/trojaundilionerg00drpf) ·
[vol. 2](https://archive.org/details/trojaundilionerg02dorp).
Already our best PD source for the Troy VI fortification plan
(`docs/TROAD-SOURCES.md:257`). As drawing technique it is architectural
plan-and-elevation, not perspective — flat, hachured, scaled. It teaches the citadel
wall's actual footprint, not how to picture it inhabited; that step belongs to the
next tradition.

### 20th-century archaeological reconstruction illustration

**Alan Sorrell (1904–1974) and the tradition he founded** — reconstruction drawings
of British and classical sites as inhabited places, published in the *Illustrated
London News* and site guidebooks from the 1930s on. **In copyright** (d. 1974;
protected to 2044 in the UK and under URAA in the US) — study only, no tracing.
[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Sorrell) ·
[Art UK survey](https://artuk.org/discover/stories/the-neo-romantic-works-of-alan-sorrell) ·
[Antiquaries Journal, "Alan Sorrell and the negotiation of the archaeological
record"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquaries-journal/article/abs/reconstruction-art-and-disciplinary-practice-alan-sorrell-and-the-negotiation-of-the-archaeological-record/6B3F279D5F55105849057D14AACC23B3).
Sorrell's signature is the elevated, oblique bird's-eye over a whole site, usually
under dramatic weather (storm light, low sun), worked as archaeologist-vetted
consultation drawings — he sent drafts back to the excavator for correction. He did
not draw Troy specifically, but the *mode* — inhabited, weather-lit, aerial, checked
against the dig — is exactly the register a "living Troy" plate needs and that our
DEM render currently lacks (weather, light direction, life).

### Modern reconstruction illustration and concept art

**Christoph Haußner, reconstruction paintings of Troy VI** (contemporary, Troy
Museum / various publications). **In copyright** — study only.
[Search context via Pinterest attribution](https://www.pinterest.com/pin/reconstruction-of-ancient-troy-vi-by-christoph-haussner--3096293478295881/).
Full-colour, photorealistic bird's-eye of the citadel — the direct successor to
Sorrell's mode, and useful chiefly as a *warning*: it is exactly as uniformly
rendered as our plate, because it is aimed at museum-panel realism rather than
selective drawing. Confirms the diagnosis rather than solving it.

**Brian Delf, Osprey Fortress 17: *Troy c. 1700–1250 BC*** (Osprey Publishing,
Gordon/Fields text). **In copyright** — study only.
[Osprey author page](https://www.ospreypublishing.com/uk/author/brian-delf/).
Delf's convention (typical of Osprey's Fortress/Campaign series) is the cutaway: full
structural detail on the fortification itself, and the surrounding landscape reduced
to flat, low-saturation colour with no independent incident. Detail and colour
*budget* are spent entirely on the one subject the page exists to explain; the plain
gets a colour and a horizon line, nothing more. This is the cleanest modern example
of detail triage by editorial purpose rather than by what a renderer *can* draw.

## Ranked shortlist: the five to eight worth this project's attention

1. **Elsheimer, *The Burning of Troy*** (c. 1600, Alte Pinakothek). PD-US. [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Adam_Elsheimer_-_The_Burning_of_Troy_-_WGA7505.jpg).
   Teaches: let one light source or one focal event set the value structure, and let
   everything outside its reach go dark and unresolved. We have no "fire" but we have
   a city and a beach — one of them can own the light.
2. **Harris/Pope, "Troja cum locis pertingentibus"** (1716). PD-US. [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:THE_ILLIAD_OF_HOMER_(translated_by_POPE)_p2.012_Troia.jpg).
   Teaches: the fleet is a *rank*, drawn once and repeated, not 459 individual hulls.
   Solves our ships problem directly, and is already this project's acknowledged
   ancestor.
3. **Tiepolo, *Building of the Trojan Horse*** (c. 1760, National Gallery). PD-US.
   [Record](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/giovanni-domenico-tiepolo-the-building-of-the-trojan-horse).
   Teaches: a small, distant city can be one silhouette edge plus one accent detail —
   it does not need walls, gates, and towers all resolved to read as a city.
4. **Choiseul-Gouffier, *Voyage pittoresque*, Troad volume** (1809, atlas 1822). PD-US.
   [Gallica](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5698421z).
   Teaches (pending a closer look John or a follow-up lane should take): this is the
   founding topographic-picture tradition of this exact landscape, worth a dedicated
   plate-by-plate pass before we finalize ground treatment.
5. **Flaxman/Piroli, *Iliad* outlines** (1793). PD-US. [Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/337355).
   Teaches, by absence: blank paper reads as "undrawn ground," which is more honest
   and more legible than half-hearted texture. If we can't commit to a real ground
   treatment somewhere in the frame, leaving it plain beats faking it.
6. **Dörpfeld, *Troja und Ilion*** (1902). PD-US. [vol. 1](https://archive.org/details/trojaundilionerg00drpf), [vol. 2](https://archive.org/details/trojaundilionerg02dorp).
   Teaches: the one place in our plate that *can* bear real linear detail — the
   citadel wall footprint — has an actual surveyed source; spend render budget there
   deliberately, not everywhere equally.
7. **Alan Sorrell tradition** (in copyright, study only). [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Sorrell), [Antiquaries Journal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquaries-journal/article/abs/reconstruction-art-and-disciplinary-practice-alan-sorrell-and-the-negotiation-of-the-archaeological-record/6B3F279D5F55105849057D14AACC23B3).
   Teaches: weather and light direction are what make a reconstruction feel like a
   moment rather than a diagram. Our plate has neither.
8. **Delf/Osprey Fortress 17: Troy** (in copyright, study only). [Osprey](https://www.ospreypublishing.com/uk/author/brian-delf/).
   Teaches: detail budget should follow editorial purpose (what the plate exists to
   explain), not renderer capability. Decide what this plate is *for* before deciding
   what gets fine linework.

## Concrete, implementable lessons for a vector renderer

1. **Give the fleet a repeating glyph, not 459 hulls.** Pope's plate and the whole
   tradition after it treat the beached fleet as a rank — one hull silhouette,
   repeated at a fixed pitch, denser or sparser to carry contingent boundaries. Stop
   rendering individual hull geometry; render a rank pattern with 2–3 tone variants
   (near/mid/far) and let repetition do the work the poem's own "black ships" epithet
   already does.
2. **Budget a light source and let it fall off.** Elsheimer and Sorrell both organize
   the whole image around one light direction with real falloff — near-full value
   range at the focal point, compressed and desaturated at distance. Our DEM shading
   is even and shadowless by comparison; picking one low sun angle and letting
   contrast collapse toward the picture's edges would do more than any texture pass.
3. **Let the city be a silhouette plus one accent, not a fully resolved elevation.**
   Tiepolo's Troy is a wall-top line and a spire. At the scale our plate puts the
   citadel on the page, full wall-course and gate detail reads as noise; one strong
   silhouette edge (the acropolis rise) plus one or two accented verticals (a
   megaron roof-line, a gate tower) will read as "a city" better than uniform detail
   does.
4. **Spend real linework only where a real source exists.** Dörpfeld's surveyed
   citadel plan is the one place in the frame with actual data resolution to justify
   fine line; the rest of the plain (per `docs/TROAD-CARTOGRAPHY.md`) is schematic
   camp-order, not surveyed ground. Detail density should track *evidence* density,
   which conveniently also tracks where a reader's eye should go.
5. **Decide the plate's one job before deciding its detail budget.** Delf's cutaways
   spend nearly all their line and colour on the one structure the page exists to
   explain, and flatten everything else to a colour and a horizon. If this plate's
   job is "orient the reader in the camp order," the camp and the two headlands earn
   detail; the DEM terrain in between does not.

**On the "selective versus uniform" diagnosis:** it holds up, and every tradition
surveyed confirms it a different way — Elsheimer by light, Tiepolo by silhouette,
Pope by glyph repetition, Delf by editorial purpose. The one modern tradition that
*doesn't* confirm it (Haußner's photorealistic Troy VI) is uniformly rendered for
exactly the same reason ours is: it optimizes for museum-panel realism rather than
for directing an eye, and it looks the same kind of finished-but-flat as our plate
does. Refinement worth adding: the mechanism isn't just "detail here, none there" —
each solved example ties its selectivity to something legible (a light source, an
editorial subject, an evidence tier), not to taste alone. Pick the organizing
principle first; the detail placement follows from it.
