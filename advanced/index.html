Advanced search

Ways to ask the text a question a plain word search cannot answer. You need no Greek to use most of them. Every number on this page comes from the 199,076 Greek words this site searches: 111,887 in the Iliad, 87,189 in the Odyssey. Check any of them against the search itself.

First, one thing worth knowing

Greek changes the end of a word to show its job in the sentence, so a search for one spelling misses most of the places that word stands. Several of the tools below exist to fix that. You can also type Greek in plain letters (Beta Code) if you have no Greek keyboard: the search page has a key to it, and a Greek keyboard of its own.

How the results are counted

Results are counted and grouped by scene, the same scenes the reader pages by, each headed by its lines and its summary. The large number above the results is the scenes; beside it, the hits. Pages break between scenes, so a scene is never split across two.

All words means every word in one scene, not anywhere in the book. Any word means at least one of them; Exact phrase, the words in a row, in order.

Each hit links to its line in the reader. Show the whole scene opens the scene a hit stands in, Greek and English, with the hits marked.

Every form of a word

Use this when you want one word wherever it stands, in whatever form it takes.

The Greek box has a setting for this. Any form of this word, the default, finds every form of the word you type: θάλασσα finds θαλάσσης and θάλασσαν. Only as I typed it matches that one spelling and no other.

Where a form could belong to two words, turn on Lemma beside the box: type the dictionary word, pick it from the list, and the search finds that word alone, in all its forms. Try every form of ἔπος.

A phrase can be widened the same way. After a search for an exact phrase, a button offers to find it again with each word allowed to take any of its forms.

Wildcards

Use this when you know part of a word but not all of it.

Type * to stand for any run of letters, or ? for exactly one letter. Both work in the middle of a word as well as at the end: pod* finds every word beginning ποδ-, and an?r finds ἀνήρ without the accent mattering.

One exception: a * at the very start of a word is not a wildcard. In plain-letter Greek it marks a capital, so *a)xilleu/s means Ἀχιλλεύς and the search reads it as that one word.

Proximity

Use this to find where two words, phrases, or kinds of word fall close together.

Proximity, beside the Search button, opens a term for each thing you are looking for. A term can be a word in any form, a word as typed, an exact phrase, or a word by grammar (below), and you can mix them freely. Each term after the first is joined to the one before by and or or, and stands near, before, after, or not near an earlier term.

Choose how close counts as near: within a number of words, or in the same line. In this order asks for the terms in the order you listed them. A match may run across the edge of a scene; turn off Keep hits that cross a scene to hold it to one. Try πούς within three words of ὠκύς.

Grammar

Use this to find a kind of word rather than a particular spelling.

Every Greek word here carries a grammatical description: case, number, gender, tense, mood, voice, person, and so on. In Proximity, set a term to by grammar and choose the description instead of typing a word. You need not know how the matching forms are spelled.

A description on its own (every plural, say) matches too much of the text to be a real question, so it needs something to narrow it. Paired with a word it becomes a question: this word near a verb in a past tense. Or choose one poem and one book under In, and ask what a whole book does with one form.

Here is the second kind at work. The dual is the form Greek uses for exactly two, and Homer uses it where later Greek would not. In Iliad 9 it makes trouble: Agamemnon sends three envoys to Achilles, yet as they go the poem speaks of them in the dual, as though they were two. Readers have argued over it since antiquity. You can go and look: every dual form in Iliad 9.

What comes back is not a list of duals, and the page says so. Many of those words could be dual and could equally be something else, since the ending does not always decide. A hit the description does not settle is marked with the readings it allows. The certain ones are worth your attention: they are the forms the old quarrel is about. By grammar, under Group by, sorts the hits by their readings.

There is no setting for "noun" or "verb" alone. Some words in Greek do the work of both at once, and nothing in the description tells them apart, so offering that choice would mean guessing.

Speeches

Use this to hold a search to the words of the poem's speakers, or of one of them.

After a search, Speech under Filter offers Speeches only, the words said inside any speech, and Spoken by, the words of one speaker. Try θεός in the mouth of Achilles.

The speeches and their speakers come from DICES (Digital Index of Classical Epic Speech, CC BY 4.0). Speeches are marked by line, so an English word is held to the line its translation puts it at.

English, and both together

Use this to search the translations, or the Greek and the English at once.

The English box searches all four translations: Murray, Butler, Pope, and the Kosmos Society revision of Butler. It takes the same three settings as the Greek box (all words, any word, exact phrase), and Any form of this word (ship finds ships) or Only as I typed it. A hyphenated query such as rosy-fingered is read as its words in a row.

With both boxes filled, Hits match says how they join. and/or, the default, finds either: the Greek, the English, or both. and keeps only the scenes that hold both, anywhere in the scene; or keeps the scenes that hold one and not the other. Under the count, the scenes are split three ways: Greek and English, Greek only, English only. Try θάλασσα and sea.

Narrowing and showing the results

Filter narrows the hits in hand: to one poem, one translation, the speeches, some of the Greek forms found, or some grammatical readings. Where English was searched, each poem is listed with its scenes and each translation under it with its hits, and Phrases lists the three-word runs around the English hits, five or more, each with a box to exclude it. View sets how they are shown: Group by work, form, or grammar; Sort in line order, by form, or commonest form first; Context of one line or three; and rows Per page, 20, 40, or 100. At 100 each row is its citation and the words around its first hit, and opens in place.

In, beside the boxes, chooses both poems or one, and then a book; the search runs on that alone.

The search in the address

The address of a results page holds the whole search, so it can be bookmarked, shared, or saved under Saved searches. Only what differs from the defaults is written, and the join of a Greek and English search always.

Links from the old search page still work: so=1 reads as spo=1, gr as a grammar term, and citations only (ctx=c, ps=c) as the 100 page.

The phrase index

Use this when you do not yet know what to look for.

Search answers "where does this occur?" The phrase index answers a different question: what does Homer repeat? It lists every run of two to five words that recurs anywhere in the two poems: 68,550 of them as printed, 158,973 when different endings of the same words count together, and 118,695 in the English translation.

This differs from the epithet-formulas and the repeated lines shown elsewhere on this site. Those list phrases a scholar has judged significant, matched exactly and within one verse. The phrase index is a plainer tool: it counts anything that recurs twice or more, however it is spelled, and lets a run of words cross from one verse into the next unless you ask it not to, a setting worth using, since Homeric repetition mostly falls within one verse.

Click any phrase to see every place it occurs, each one linked to its passage in the reader.

What these tools cannot tell you

  • Searches ignore accents unless you turn accent matching on, so λόγος and a differently accented spelling of the same letters count as one word by default.
  • The translations are not lined up word for word with the Greek. Each translator marks his place every few lines, and an English word near the edge of a scene is placed by estimate between those marks.
  • None of these tools reads Greek. They match forms and count them. What a passage means remains your work.
